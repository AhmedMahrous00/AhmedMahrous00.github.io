1 Introduction

Many learning systems, once deployed, change the environment that later generates their training data. A credit scoring model can change how applicants behave; an algorithmic trading strategy can move the price data on which future strategies are trained. This feedback effect is the focus of performative prediction. Existing theory largely studies the case of one learner facing the consequences of its own deployment. Many real systems, however, contain multiple learners whose deployments jointly shape the environment.

The multi-agent setting is not simply a larger version of the single-agent problem; it introduces different difficulties. Feedback can travel across agents: one agent’s deployment changes the data distribution, which changes another agent’s retraining problem, which may itself affect the data distribution or other agents. Stability therefore depends not only on the overall strength of performativity, but also on how feedback is routed through the agent network.

We ask whether there is a general threshold separating stable multi-agent performative learning from unavoidable learning failure. When does repeated retraining converge to a stable equilibrium?

We answer this question through a reflexivity matrix Γ\Gamma with entries Γi​j:=ai​j/μi\Gamma_{ij}:=a_{ij}/\mu_{i}, measuring how strongly agent jj’s deployment can move agent ii’s best response, after normalizing by the curvature of agent ii’s objective. Thus Γ\Gamma records who affects whom, and how strongly, in the performative feedback network.

Our main result is a phase transition at ρ⁡(Γ)=1\rho(\Gamma)=1, where ρ⁡(Γ)\rho(\Gamma) is the spectral radius of Γ\Gamma. This quantity captures the largest asymptotic amplification factor of the feedback network. If ρ⁡(Γ)<1\rho(\Gamma)<1, cross-agent feedback dissipates: repeated retraining is a contraction in a Perron–Frobenius weighted norm, converges geometrically to a unique performatively stable equilibrium, and incurs bounded cumulative excess loss. Above the threshold, this positive guarantee cannot be extended. We construct supercritical systems in which every decentralized local-information learner suffers cumulative loss that grows linearly with time, regardless of the retraining rule. Under sensitivity and curvature assumptions, the spectral radius of the reflexivity matrix gives a sharp boundary for general stability guarantees: below one, all systems satisfying our assumptions contract, while above one, there exist supercritical systems for which decentralized learning incurs linear cumulative loss.

Contributions.

First, we introduce the reflexivity matrix Γ\Gamma, a nonnegative matrix whose entry Γi​j\Gamma_{ij} records how strongly agent jj’s deployment can move agent ii’s best response, as determined by per-agent gradient sensitivities and curvature constants. We prove an entrywise best-response sensitivity bound that reduces nonlinear retraining dynamics with general strongly convex losses to a spectral comparison through Γ\Gamma, making Perron–Frobenius theory available. Second, we prove the subcritical side of the threshold: if ρ⁡(Γ)<1\rho(\Gamma)<1, repeated retraining is a contraction in a Perron–Frobenius weighted norm, yielding a unique performatively stable equilibrium, geometric convergence from any initialization, and cumulative dynamic excess loss bounded uniformly in the time horizon. Third, we prove the supercritical side: for a rank-one family with ρ⁡(Γ)=1+ε\rho(\Gamma)=1+\varepsilon, every decentralized local-information learner incurs cumulative excess loss Ω⁡(Δ2​T)\Omega(\Delta^{2}T) over horizons T=O⁡(1/ε)T=O(1/\varepsilon). Together, these results identify ρ⁡(Γ)=1\rho(\Gamma)=1 as a boundary for general stability guarantees under primitive gradient-sensitivity and curvature assumptions, and recover the single-agent scalar threshold from prior work when k=1k=1.

2 Related Work

This paper draws on three lines of research. The first is the single-agent theory of performative prediction, which introduces the framework we extend. The second is the multi-agent theory of performative prediction and decision-dependent games, which is where our contribution is most directly positioned. The third is the combined tradition of spectral methods for network games and statistical lower bound techniques, from which we import two standard technical tools.

Performative prediction.

Perdomo et al. (2020) introduced performative prediction in a single-agent setting, formalizing performative stability and convergence of repeated retraining under sensitivity conditions. Subsequent work largely retained this single-agent viewpoint while extending the framework to stochastic, causal, nonconvex, and sequential settings (Mendler-Dünner et al., 2020; Miller et al., 2021; Brown et al., 2022; Hardt et al., 2022; Mofakhami et al., 2023; Mandal et al., 2023). Closest to our threshold result, Anagnostides et al. (2026) established hardness and lower bounds at the single-agent scalar threshold. We depart from this line by studying decentralized multi-agent retraining, where performative feedback is cross-agent and the scalar threshold is replaced by the spectral condition ρ⁡(Γ)=1\rho(\Gamma)=1.

Multi-agent performativity.

The multi-agent extension is the most direct predecessor of our work, and the literature divides into three strands.

The first establishes scalar contraction conditions for convergence. Narang et al. (2023) formalized nn-player performative prediction with a scalar aggregate threshold ρ<1\rho<1, and analogous scalar conditions appear in cooperative consensus learning (Li et al., 2022), decentralized games with coupled constraints (Yan and Cao, 2024), and a Wasserstein-free variant (Zhong et al., 2025). We depart from this strand on two axes. We replace the scalar aggregate with a matrix Γ\Gamma that preserves per-agent-pair feedback, and we pair the upper bound with a worst-case information-theoretic lower bound above the threshold.

The second develops spectral characterizations in special models. Wang et al. (2023) derived a necessary-and-sufficient spectral condition for a linear-quadratic mean-squared-error model on multiplex networks. Their spectral object is a weighted adjacency matrix with exogenous structure, and the condition is specific to the linear-quadratic model. Our Γ\Gamma is built from primitive per-agent quantities, applies to general strongly convex losses, and is paired with a lower bound.

The third identifies dynamical phase transitions. Piliouras and Yu (2023) proved a transition from stability to Li–Yorke chaos as update aggressiveness grows under scalar aggregate influence. Their threshold is dynamical and concerns the long-run behavior of specific update rules; ours is information-theoretic and applies to every decentralized local-information learner. Farina and Perdomo (2026) show that, for bounded losses, no-regret online learning yields approximate performative stability of a mixture over joint deployment histories, without regularity assumptions on the distribution maps. Our subcritical result guarantees geometric convergence of the last retraining iterate to a unique performatively stable equilibrium under curvature and sensitivity assumptions.

Spectral methods and lower bounds.

Two technical traditions supply tools used in our analysis without serving as competing frameworks. Spectral conditions for equilibrium uniqueness and stability are well established in network games (Ballester et al., 2006; Bramoullé et al., 2014; Parise and Ozdaglar, 2019), where the spectral radius governs payoff coupling: agent jj’s action enters agent ii’s utility directly. In our setting, it governs distributional feedback through D⁡(w)D(w), and Γ\Gamma is built from gradient sensitivities and curvature constants rather than payoff coefficients. Our lower bound uses the two-point method (Tsybakov, 2009); recent extensions to interactive (Chen et al., 2024) and decision-dependent (Cutler et al., 2024) settings do not cover our performative learning setting. Our construction is tailored to multi-agent performative feedback over a finite horizon.

3 Model

This section specifies the multi-agent performative prediction model used throughout the paper. We describe the agents, their objectives, the dynamics of repeated retraining, and the assumptions that underlie the reduction of Section 4.

There are kk agents. Each agent i∈{1,…,k}i\in\{1,\dots,k\} chooses a deployment wiw_{i} from a feasible set 𝒲i=ℝdi\mathcal{W}_{i}=\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}. The joint deployment profile is w:=(w1,…,wk)∈𝒲:=𝒲1×⋯×𝒲kw:=(w_{1},\dots,w_{k})\in\mathcal{W}:=\mathcal{W}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{W}_{k}. The deployed profile induces a shared endogenous distribution D⁡(w)D(w) over observations z∈𝒵z\in\mathcal{Z}. This distribution represents the data-generating process that all agents face after the profile ww has been deployed. The dependence of DD on ww captures the performative nature of the system: the environment that agents observe is shaped by their own collective actions.

Each agent ii has a loss function ℓi:𝒲i×𝒵→ℝ\ell_{i}:\mathcal{W}_{i}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R} and aims to minimize its performative risk

Fi​(ui,w):=𝔼z∼D⁡(w)​[ℓi​(ui,z)].F_{i}(u_{i};w)\;:=\;\mathbb{E}_{z\sim D(w)}\big[\ell_{i}(u_{i};z)\big]. (1)

The two arguments of FiF_{i} play distinct roles. The first, uiu_{i}, is the candidate action optimized by agent ii. The second, ww, is the deployed profile that determines the environment through D⁡(w)D(w). Retraining couples these roles by replacing the deployed profile with agents’ optimized actions.

For each agent ii and each deployed profile ww, the best response is

Bi​(w):=arg​minui∈𝒲i⁡Fi​(ui,w).B_{i}(w)\;:=\;\argmin_{u_{i}\in\mathcal{W}_{i}}F_{i}(u_{i};w). (2)

Assumption 1 below ensures that this minimizer exists and is unique. The joint best-response map is B⁡(w):=(B1​(w),…,Bk​(w))B(w):=\big(B_{1}(w),\dots,B_{k}(w)\big). An equilibrium of the system is a profile that is consistent with every agent’s best response.

Definition 1 (Performatively stable equilibrium).

A profile w⋆∈𝒲w^{\star}\in\mathcal{W} is a performatively stable equilibrium if w⋆=B⁡(w⋆)w^{\star}=B(w^{\star}).

At a performatively stable equilibrium, each agent’s deployed action is optimal for the distribution induced by the joint deployment. Existence of such an equilibrium is not assumed; it is derived in Section 5 as a consequence of the spectral condition on the reflexivity matrix.

We study the repeated retraining dynamics in which every agent simultaneously replaces its current deployment with its best response,

wt+1=B(wt),t=0,1,2,…,w_{t+1}=B(w_{t}),\qquad t=0,1,2,\dots, (3)

with initialization w0∈𝒲w_{0}\in\mathcal{W}. The central question of the paper is when the sequence {wt}\{w_{t}\} converges to a performatively stable equilibrium, and when convergence fails.

Main assumptions.

Three assumptions are used throughout the paper. The first two are local to individual agents and enter the reduction of Section 4. The third is a mild regularity condition on the feasible sets that allows application of Banach’s fixed-point theorem.

Assumption 1 (Strong convexity in own action).

For each agent ii and every fixed deployed profile w∈𝒲w\in\mathcal{W}, the map ui↦Fi​(ui,w)u_{i}\mapsto F_{i}(u_{i};w) is μi\mu_{i}-strongly convex on 𝒲i\mathcal{W}_{i}, with μi>0\mu_{i}>0.

Strong convexity makes Bi​(w)B_{i}(w) unique and quantifies agent ii’s resistance to gradient perturbations. Larger μi\mu_{i} means that the best response is less sensitive to changes in the data distribution.

Assumption 2 (Gradient sensitivity to the deployed profile).

For each ii, FiF_{i} is continuously differentiable in uiu_{i}, and there exist nonnegative constants ai​j≥0a_{ij}\geq 0 for all i,j∈{1,…,k}i,j\in\{1,\dots,k\} such that for every agent ii, every candidate action ui∈𝒲iu_{i}\in\mathcal{W}_{i}, and every pair of deployed profiles w,w′∈𝒲w,w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W},

‖∇uiFi​(ui,w)−∇uiFi​(ui,w′)‖≤∑j=1kai​j​‖wj−wj′‖.\|\nabla_{u_{i}}F_{i}(u_{i};w)-\nabla_{u_{i}}F_{i}(u_{i};w^{\prime})\|\;\leq\;\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{ij}\,\|w_{j}-w_{j}^{\prime}\|.

Gradient sensitivity quantifies how a change in the deployed profile affects each agent’s first-order condition. The coefficient ai​ja_{ij} measures how much agent jj’s deployment disturbs agent ii’s gradient. If agent jj has no effect on agent ii’s optimization problem, then ai​j=0a_{ij}=0. Larger values of ai​ja_{ij} indicate stronger cross-agent coupling. The diagonal coefficient ai​ia_{ii} captures agent ii’s own self-reflexive effect through the environment.

Assumptions 1 and 2 are the only two channels through which agents interact mathematically in this paper. Every downstream result is derived from these two assumptions and their combination in Section 4. The reflexivity matrix Γ\Gamma of Section 4 is built from exactly the parameters {μi}\{\mu_{i}\} and {ai​j}\{a_{ij}\} introduced here.

Assumption 3 (Unconstrained domains).

Each feasible set is 𝒲i=ℝdi\mathcal{W}_{i}=\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}.

This makes 𝒲\mathcal{W} complete under the norms used below, enabling Banach’s fixed-point theorem, and ensures that retraining stays in 𝒲\mathcal{W}.

4 The Reduction: From Retraining Dynamics to Γ\Gamma

The retraining dynamics wt+1=B⁡(wt)w_{t+1}=B(w_{t}) are nonlinear and couple all kk agents through the shared endogenous distribution D⁡(w)D(w). Direct analysis of these dynamics is difficult without additional structure. This section introduces the reduction used throughout the paper: under the assumptions of Section 3, changes in the nonlinear map BB are bounded entrywise by a nonnegative matrix Γ\Gamma. The spectral properties of Γ\Gamma can therefore be used to bound the dynamics of BB.

The first step bounds the shift in any agent’s best response induced by a shift in the deployed profile. The bound is linear and decomposes cleanly across agents.

Theorem 2 (Best-response sensitivity).

Under Assumptions 1–2 and 3, for every agent ii and every pair of deployed profiles w,w′w,w^{\prime},

‖Bi​(w)−Bi​(w′)‖≤∑j=1kai​jμi​‖wj−wj′‖.\|B_{i}(w)-B_{i}(w^{\prime})\|\;\leq\;\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{a_{ij}}{\mu_{i}}\,\|w_{j}-w_{j}^{\prime}\|.
Proof.

Fix an agent ii and let ui:=Bi​(w)u_{i}:=B_{i}(w) and ui′:=Bi​(w′)u_{i}^{\prime}:=B_{i}(w^{\prime}). First-order optimality gives ∇uiFi​(ui,w)=0\nabla_{u_{i}}F_{i}(u_{i};w)=0 and ∇uiFi​(ui′,w′)=0\nabla_{u_{i}}F_{i}(u_{i}^{\prime};w^{\prime})=0. Strong convexity of Fi​(⋅,w)F_{i}(\cdot;w) (Assumption 1) yields μi​‖ui−ui′‖≤‖∇uiFi​(ui,w)−∇uiFi​(ui′,w)‖\mu_{i}\|u_{i}-u_{i}^{\prime}\|\leq\|\nabla_{u_{i}}F_{i}(u_{i};w)-\nabla_{u_{i}}F_{i}(u_{i}^{\prime};w)\|. Substituting the first optimality condition and then the second gives μi​‖ui−ui′‖≤‖∇uiFi​(ui′,w)‖=‖∇uiFi​(ui′,w)−∇uiFi​(ui′,w′)‖\mu_{i}\|u_{i}-u_{i}^{\prime}\|\leq\|\nabla_{u_{i}}F_{i}(u_{i}^{\prime};w)\|=\|\nabla_{u_{i}}F_{i}(u_{i}^{\prime};w)-\nabla_{u_{i}}F_{i}(u_{i}^{\prime};w^{\prime})\|. The right-hand side is a gradient difference at a fixed action ui′u_{i}^{\prime} across two deployed profiles, which Assumption 2 bounds by ∑jai​j​‖wj−wj′‖\sum_{j}a_{ij}\,\|w_{j}-w_{j}^{\prime}\|. Dividing by μi>0\mu_{i}>0 yields the claim. ∎

The proof uses each structural assumption once: gradient sensitivity bounds the performative change in agent ii’s gradient, and strong convexity converts it into a best-response change. Their interaction appears only through ai​j/μia_{ij}/\mu_{i}, the ratio of agent jj’s disturbance to agent ii’s curvature, which measures how much jj can shift ii’s best response.

The weights in Theorem 2 define the central object of the paper.

Definition 3 (Reflexivity matrix).

The reflexivity matrix is the k×kk\times k nonnegative matrix Γ\Gamma with entries Γi​j:=ai​j/μi\Gamma_{ij}:=a_{ij}/\mu_{i}, for i,j=1,…,ki,j=1,\dots,k.

In terms of Γ\Gamma, Theorem 2 states that for every ii and every w,w′w,w^{\prime},

‖Bi​(w)−Bi​(w′)‖≤∑j=1kΓi​j​‖wj−wj′‖.\|B_{i}(w)-B_{i}(w^{\prime})\|\;\leq\;\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Gamma_{ij}\,\|w_{j}-w_{j}^{\prime}\|. (4)

The vector of best-response shifts is therefore dominated entrywise by the product of Γ\Gamma with the vector of action shifts.

The matrix Γ\Gamma is the central reduction object. It is built from primitive per-agent quantities rather than imposed as an aggregate Lipschitz constant, and its nonnegativity makes Perron–Frobenius theory available for the weighted-norm contraction argument below. The rank-one construction in Section 6 saturates (4) along the Perron direction, so the same spectral radius governs both the upper and lower sides of the threshold.

(2023), consider scalar agents with quadratic losses ℓi​(ui,zi)=12​(ui−zi)2\ell_{i}(u_{i};z_{i})=\tfrac{1}{2}(u_{i}-z_{i})^{2} and deterministic observations z=C​wz=Cw, where C≥0C\geq 0. Here μi=1\mu_{i}=1, the data-gradient Lipschitz constants are βi=1\beta_{i}=1, and the full-profile Wasserstein sensitivities are γi=∥Ci:∥2\gamma_{i}=\|C_{i:}\|_{2}. 5 Subcritical Regime: Contraction and Bounded Excess Loss

This section proves the positive side of the threshold. Under the assumptions of Section 3, if ρ⁡(Γ)<1\rho(\Gamma)<1, then the retraining map is a contraction in a Perron–Frobenius weighted norm. This yields a unique performatively stable equilibrium, geometric convergence from any initialization, and a cumulative excess-loss bound that does not grow with the time horizon.

Contraction of the state dynamics alone does not give a performance bound. To control cumulative excess loss, we add smoothness in each agent’s own action.

Assumption 4 (Smoothness in own action).

For each agent ii and every fixed deployed profile ww, the map ui↦Fi​(ui,w)u_{i}\mapsto F_{i}(u_{i};w) is LiL_{i}-smooth.

The natural benchmark in a time-varying environment is the current best response. At round tt, agent ii deploys wi,tw_{i,t}, while the action minimizing its risk under the current profile wtw_{t} is Bi​(wt)B_{i}(w_{t}). The gap between these two actions is the instantaneous excess loss, and summing over agents and rounds gives

Reg~T:=∑t=0T−1∑i=1k(Fi​(wi,t,wt)−Fi​(Bi​(wt),wt)).\widetilde{\mathrm{Reg}}_{T}\;:=\;\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Big(F_{i}(w_{i,t};w_{t})-F_{i}(B_{i}(w_{t});w_{t})\Big). (6)

This benchmark compares each deployment to the best response in the environment it actually faces. It also avoids assumptions on how objective values vary with the deployed profile. Bounds against the fixed equilibrium comparator require such assumptions and are therefore treated separately.

6 Supercritical Regime

Section 5 established that ρ⁡(Γ)<1\rho(\Gamma)<1 is sufficient for geometric convergence and bounded cumulative excess loss. Whether the supercritical regime produces an intrinsic learning obstruction is delicate: failure of contraction does not by itself imply information-theoretic impossibility, since with enough observations a learner may recover the correct equilibrium even when repeated retraining diverges. We show that the threshold has a sharp negative side in the worst case. For every ε>0\varepsilon>0, we construct a supercritical family with ρ⁡(Γ)=1+ε\rho(\Gamma)=1+\varepsilon in which any decentralized local-information learner - one that at each round uses only its own history of observations and actions - incurs linear cumulative excess loss for T=O⁡(1/ε)T=O(1/\varepsilon) rounds.

All agents know the model parameters (Γε,Δ,σε2,v)(\Gamma_{\varepsilon},\Delta,\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2},v); only the hidden parameter ν\nu is unknown.

Definition 7 (Decentralized local-information learner).

A decentralized local-information learner is a policy profile A=(A1,…,Ak)A=(A_{1},\dots,A_{k}) in which agent ii’s action wi,tw_{i,t} at round tt is measurable with respect to the private history ℋi,tpre:=σ(wi,0:t−1,Yi,0:t−1,Ui,0:t)\mathcal{H}_{i,t}^{\mathrm{pre}}:=\sigma\big(w_{i,0:t-1},\;Y_{i,0:t-1},\;U_{i,0:t}\big), where Yi,sY_{i,s} is agent ii’s private observation at round ss and Ui,sU_{i,s} is agent ii’s internal randomization.

No agent observes the full system state: each sees only its own signal, without access to other agents’ actions or observations. The lower bound applies to any learner satisfying this constraint, regardless of the algorithm used within each agent.

Hard family.

The construction places all endogenous amplification along a single direction, which simplifies the analysis and makes the separation between the two environments explicit.

Fix ε∈(0,1]\varepsilon\in(0,1] and a unit vector v∈ℝkv\in\mathbb{R}^{k} with nonnegative entries. Define the reflexivity matrix

Γε:=(1+ε)​v​v⊤.\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\;:=\;(1+\varepsilon)\,vv^{\top}. (8)

The matrix Γε\Gamma_{\varepsilon} is nonnegative, symmetric, and rank one, with single nonzero eigenvalue 1+ε1+\varepsilon, so ρ⁡(Γε)=1+ε\rho(\Gamma_{\varepsilon})=1+\varepsilon. The system is therefore supercritical by construction, with ε\varepsilon measuring the distance above the threshold. Each agent has di=1d_{i}=1 and 𝒲i=ℝ\mathcal{W}_{i}=\mathbb{R}, and the quadratic loss gives μi=1\mu_{i}=1, so the abstract reflexivity matrix of Section 4 coincides with Γε\Gamma_{\varepsilon} in this construction.

The environment contains a hidden binary parameter ν∈{−1,+1}\nu\in\{-1,+1\} that determines the true state of the world. Fix an amplitude Δ>0\Delta>0 and let the noise variance be σε2:=8​Δ2/ε\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}:=8\Delta^{2}/\varepsilon. The observation model is

Yt=ν​Δ​v+Γε​wt+ξt,ξt∼𝒩⁡(0,σε2​Ik),Y_{t}\;=\;\nu\Delta v+\Gamma_{\varepsilon}w_{t}+\xi_{t},\qquad\xi_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}I_{k}), (9)

where each agent ii observes only the coordinate Yi,tY_{i,t}.

Minimizing over aa gives the best-response map B⁡(w,ν)=ν​Δ​v+Γε​wB(w;\nu)=\nu\Delta v+\Gamma_{\varepsilon}w. The two equilibria w⋆,+w^{\star,+} and w⋆,−w^{\star,-} are therefore located at distance 2​Δ/ε2\Delta/\varepsilon along vv. As ε\varepsilon decreases, these equilibria move apart, and the geometric penalty for choosing the wrong sign grows. This divergence is the mechanism that converts slow information acquisition into large cumulative excess loss.

The instantaneous excess performative loss at round tt is

rtν:=∑i=1k(Fi​(wi,t,wt,ν)−Fi​(wi⋆,ν,w⋆,ν,ν)),r_{t}^{\nu}\;:=\;\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Big(F_{i}(w_{i,t};w_{t},\nu)-F_{i}(w_{i}^{\star,\nu};w^{\star,\nu},\nu)\Big), (12)

The subcritical result uses a dynamic comparator (the current best response Bi​(wt)B_{i}(w_{t})), whereas (12) uses the fixed equilibrium comparator w⋆,νw^{\star,\nu}: the dynamic comparator measures one-step adaptation to the current caused environment, while the equilibrium comparator measures whether the learner identifies the correct stable equilibrium. In the supercritical construction these goals decouple, so the equilibrium comparator is the appropriate benchmark for the two-environment testing argument. For the quadratic family in (10) the two comparators in fact yield identical instantaneous excess loss, so the Ω⁡(Δ2​T)\Omega(\Delta^{2}T) bound holds under either benchmark.

The proof proceeds in three steps. Step 1 shows that the two environments ν=+1\nu=+1 and ν=−1\nu=-1 remain statistically indistinguishable over the critical horizon. Step 2 shows that misidentifying the environment forces the learner into a region of large excess loss. Step 3 combines these two facts.

The argument has two parts.

Indistinguishability. We prove the KL bound under a stronger centralized observation model that reveals the full profile wtw_{t} and vector observation YtY_{t}; the bound therefore also applies to decentralized learners. Conditional on the realized pre-observation history ℱt\mathcal{F}_{t}, the action wtw_{t} is fixed by the learner’s policy. Under the two hypotheses, the next observation is Gaussian with common covariance σε2​Ik\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}I_{k} and conditional means differing by 2​Δ​v2\Delta v. The positive theorem assumes exact population best responses, whereas the lower bound concerns noisy observations over a finite horizon, with σε2=8​Δ2/ε\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}=8\Delta^{2}/\varepsilon. The proof bounds indistinguishability even for a centralized learner observing the same signals, so communication alone does not remove this obstruction in the constructed family.

7 Discussion

Reflexivity matrix and interpretation.

The framework replaces a scalar Lipschitz constant with the reflexivity matrix Γ\Gamma, whose entries ai​j/μia_{ij}/\mu_{i} retain the per-pair structure of performative feedback. This matters because two systems with the same scalar bound can have different stability behavior if their feedback is routed through different agent networks. The Perron weights qq in Theorem 4 encode this heterogeneity by rescaling agents according to their role in the collective retraining dynamics. When k=1k=1, Γ\Gamma reduces to the scalar ratio a11/μ1a_{11}/\mu_{1}, recovering the single-agent contraction threshold of Perdomo et al. (2020). More generally, ρ⁡(Γ)=1\rho(\Gamma)=1 separates dissipative feedback, where repeated retraining contracts, from supercritical feedback, where uniform stability guarantees fail. We use reflexivity for this deployment-to-data channel, following the financial-market terminology of Soros (1987); we do not formalize the broader economic concept.

Beyond the spectral radius.

The threshold ρ⁡(Γ)=1\rho(\Gamma)=1 governs asymptotic behavior, but finite-horizon dynamics can depend on finer matrix properties and initial conditions. One important case is non-normality: ‖Γt‖\|\Gamma^{t}\| may grow transiently even if ρ⁡(Γ)<1\rho(\Gamma)<1, as illustrated by the non-normal subcritical trajectory in Panel 1(b). Thus the spectral radius identifies the asymptotic threshold, while quantities such as the numerical range or pseudospectra may better capture finite-horizon amplification. We leave this refinement to future work.

Empirical measurement.

The framework identifies a stability quantity, ρ⁡(Γ)\rho(\Gamma), that is measurable in principle but difficult to identify empirically. Each entry ai​ja_{ij} is counterfactual: it describes how agent ii’s gradient would respond to alternative deployments by agent jj, while μi\mu_{i} is also not directly observed. Estimation is therefore a causal identification problem, since common system shocks may affect both agent jj’s deployment and agent ii’s gradient. Testing whether estimated values of ρ⁡(Γ)\rho(\Gamma) predict observed instability is a concrete direction for future work. The primitive bounds are useful when BB is defined implicitly by a nonlinear optimizer: curvature and pairwise gradient sensitivity can be bounded without solving or differentiating the full best-response map. The certificate can be conservative and depends on the chosen block geometry; failure of ρ⁡(Γ)<1\rho(\Gamma)<1 alone does not diagnose instability. As a design lever, adding a fixed-reference quadratic penalty λi2​‖ui−uiref‖2\tfrac{\lambda_{i}}{2}\|u_{i}-u_{i}^{\mathrm{ref}}\|^{2} replaces Γi​j\Gamma_{ij} by ai​j/(μi+λi)a_{ij}/(\mu_{i}+\lambda_{i}), potentially restoring the certificate, while changing the objective and generally the equilibrium.

Limitations and extensions.

Several modeling choices delimit our results. The main guarantees assume strong convexity in each agent’s own action and exact best-response retraining; Appendix C controls approximate retraining under explicit error bounds. Sharper finite-sample rates, weaker curvature assumptions, and gradient updates remain directions for future work. We also treat the reflexivity matrix as fixed across rounds. If the interaction structure evolves, a natural extension is to study time-varying matrices Γt\Gamma_{t}, where tools such as joint spectral radii may be needed to characterize stability. The lower bound establishes that no universal positive guarantee can extend above the threshold, since at least one supercritical family forces linear cumulative excess loss; it does not assert that every supercritical Γ\Gamma is unlearnable. Characterizing which supercritical systems remain learnable is open for future work. Coordination among agents may shift the threshold, while damped retraining is a natural design lever for stabilizing otherwise unstable systems.
